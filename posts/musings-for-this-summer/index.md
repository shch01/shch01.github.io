I recently graduated from high school. As I think forward to the ~2.5 months until I start at Harvard, I'm realizing that I don't have much on my agenda. I think this is generally a good thing, as there is much I hope to learn before I am thrust into the rigorous first-year classes I want to take. This basically means I plan to read a whole lot of books, but I also hope to work on several informal projects to build my technical maturity in preparation for applying to research fellowships early next year. 

I'd like to fill this site with some writing detailing my half-baked thoughts as I read and explore this summer. I also want to write about some non-technical topics related to philosophy, economics, and public policy. My ideas on these topics are at most quarter-baked. But I do think there is immense value in forcing one's self to think about things coherently, which is often best done by writing out one's thoughts. 

I'd like to use this post in particular to keep a laundry list of topics I want to write more extensively about. Listed below are a few of these topics. If you want to discuss any of this, please reach out! 


**The Harvard College grade cap**

Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences [recently voted](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2026/5/20/fas-passes-a-grade-cap/) to cap the number of A grades given out at the college to 20% of class size (+4 if applicable). This policy will take effect in 2027, which will be my second year at Harvard. [Like most undergraduates](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2026/2/9/hua-grading-proposal-survey/), I personally disagree with this policy. Though I'm honestly not too worried about how it will affect me. I see the main implication as making the distinction between an A and an A- obsolete, or at least more insignificant than it already is. I, like (I presume) most of my peers in the class of 2030, would not bet on myself being in the top 20% of any class I take at Harvard (though I likely will be in some), so as far as I'm concerned, an A and an A- are the same grade. 

I understand why the faculty wants to give out fewer As, but, if anything, I would prefer if they just made their classes harder until only ~20% of students receive As. A policy like this feels easier to adapt to and less arbitrary than a hard cap. It would be similar to [the policy Princeton once had](https://www.dailyprincetonian.com/article/2004/04/grade-inflation-plan-passes), but would limit only As, not the number of A- grades. 

HBS professor [Scott Kominers](https://www.scottkom.com/) wrote a great [op ed](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2026/3/27/kominers-harvard-grade-cap/) in the Crimson opposing the grade cap, and co-authored a [game-theoretic paper](https://scottkom.com/assets/articles/Gans_r_Kominers_Informative_Grading_Requires_Cross-Course_Comparability.pdf) on the topic, which is interesting in its own right. 

To be honest, I suspect that this policy will be repealed or at least significantly altered in a few years, though I will likely have to live with it for all my years at Harvard. I know Princeton repealed its policy primarily because of unhealthy competition among students. This is something I do fear. 

Some have already pointed out that language learners [might suffer the most](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2026/3/12/russell-harvard-language-classes/) from the grade cap. I might add that, if students in study groups or those who know their peers in a class at a personal level generally perform better, another group that might become (further) disadvantaged by the grade cap are those who struggle to connect with their classmates in a particular course. Examples of these students are those dealing with social anxiety, non-native English speakers, or perhaps female students in many STEM classes with a greater proportion of male students. 

Not to mention, the policy just accentuates the differences in students' backgrounds before they get to Harvard. Students who were able to succeed at more rigorous high schools will likely get higher As, and, under the grade cap, will then  become the only people with As. This feels very unfair. 

**Books**

There are a number of books I would like to read this summer. Hopefully, after I read one of these books, or as much of it as I would like to, I will make a detailed post on this site about my thoughts. I will add my thoughts going into the books here.

These selections are not necessarily about math, physics, computer science, or philosophy. They, in my perception, sit somewhere in the middle, as books written by authors who could, if they wished, write a separate book on any of those topics. That is, they appear to me as compiled intellectual musings organized in such a way that a coherent storyline emerges. Perhaps they are just books written by physicists. 

- *Gödel, Escher, Bach* by Douglas R. Hofstadter. I've heard a lot about this book, and from what I gather, it is not necessarily about the work of any of the individuals mentioned in its title. Of course, there are *connections* between the fields of math, art, and music. However, I myself have always maintained that I do not believe there is anything inherently special about possessing knowledge in a specific field. That is, all fields, perhaps with the exception of fundamental physics, are *epistemically isomorphic* in a colloquial sense. 


- *Cybernetics* by Norbert Wiener. I've already been exposed to many of the ideas of early cybernetics. My general perception of the field is that around the 1990s, people realized that the concepts encompassed by cybernetics (my tense is intentional here) could be studied more effectively without adhering to a unifying framework (i.e., a broader context). This conclusion, I believe, has proven to be true, which is not surprising. Though this may not have been Wiener's motivation, I think that cybernetics, today, and, perhaps always, has been most interesting in a somewhat abstract systems-theoretic sense. However, cybernetics has also always struck me as a field pertinent to evolutionary technology, which will become increasingly relevant as we learn to engineer more of the world around us. 

- *Quantum Computing Since Democritus* by Scott Aaronson. I’ve listened to a number of Aaronson’s talks and podcast appearances. I’ve always found his explanations and examples to be especially compelling and intuitive. I am interested in this book specifically because I was enamored with the notion that the question of *what is computable?* has deep implications for the physical sciences. For example, in many of his talks, Aaronson gives the example that if one possessed a computer that could brute-force its way through proving a conjecture with infinitely many cases by proving every case in finite time, such a computer would require so much energy that it would exceed its Schwardchild radius and collapse into a black hole.
